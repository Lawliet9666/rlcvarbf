Learning to Adapt Risk via Differentiable CVaR Barrier Functions

Xinyi Wang1, Taekyung Kim1, Bardh Hoxha2, Georgios Fainekos2, Dimitra Panagou1,
1Department of Robotics, University of Michigan, 2Toyota North America Research & Development
Paper Video Code

TL;DR: Risk-adaptive CVaR barrier functions let a robot trade off safety and feasibility in crowded uncertain environments by adjusting its risk level online while keeping probabilistic safety guarantees.

Abstract

We propose a risk-adaptive approach based on the Conditional Value-at-Risk Barrier Function (CVaR-BF), where the risk level is automatically adjusted to accept the minimum necessary risk, ensuring CVaR safety is guaranteed at least a pre-defined threshold while improving optimization feasibility under uncertainty. Additionally, we introduce a dynamic zone-based barrier function which characterizes the collision likelihood by evaluating the relative state between the robot and the obstacle. It expands the available adjustment space for the risk level while maintaining the desired probabilistic safety guarantee. By integrating risk adaptation with this new function, our approach enables the robot to proactively avoid obstacles in highly dynamic environments.

Motivation

(a) A fixed risk level is not flexible enough: A low risk tolerance enhances safety but can render the optimization infeasible, whereas a high risk tolerance improves feasibility at the expense of safety.
(b) In highly dynamic scenarios, where obstacles move unpredictably and rapidly, the robot requires sufficient time and space to respond and adjust its risk level.
(c) Our method dynamically adjusts the risk level within an extended risk range to maintain feasibility while ensuring user-defined probabilistic safety. The robot proactively modifies its trajectory before approaching obstacles, but only when necessary, thus avoiding unnecessary conservatism.

Real-World Dataset Validation

We evaluated the proposed CVaR-BF optimization as a safety filter for RL-based methods, such as CrowdNav++ (serving as the nominal controller), on a real-world pedestrian trajectory dataset (Univ Scenario)  [1]. Experimental results demonstrate that the RL agent alone frequently failed to avoid collisions in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, where the robot radius was larger than during training. In contrast, with our safety filter, the RL agent navigated safely even in these challenging OOD settings.

Experiments & Research Questions

Accordion-style framing adapted from RoboMME, rewritten here for the CVaR-BF story in this paper.

▶ RQ1: Why adapt the risk level online instead of fixing one CVaR level?

Fixed-risk controllers expose a hard safety-versus-feasibility tradeoff. The proposed controller is designed to move along that tradeoff online instead of committing to one global risk level before the rollout starts.

  • A conservative fixed risk level can preserve safety margin but may make the optimization infeasible when the crowd closes in quickly.
  • An aggressive fixed risk level can keep the problem solvable, but it accepts unnecessary risk in easier parts of the trajectory.
  • The adaptive controller raises the accepted risk only when needed and relaxes it again once the scene becomes less constrained.

▶ RQ2: What does the dynamic zone-based barrier add in uncertain crowded scenes?

The dynamic zone-based barrier uses relative geometry and motion to characterize how threatening an obstacle is before the interaction becomes critical.

  • By enlarging the admissible adjustment window, the controller has more room to trade risk for feasibility while still respecting the user-defined safety threshold.
  • This makes it easier to react proactively in fast or unpredictable scenes instead of waiting for a last-second correction.
  • In the rollout sweeps, the intended effect is earlier and smoother rerouting when uncertainty or crowd density increases.

▶ RQ3: How does the proposed controller compare with classical filters and learned baselines?

The interactive rollout gallery places RCBF, CBF, fixed-risk CVaR, ORCA, CrowdNav, CrowdNav++, and the adaptive controller on shared reference scenes so the tradeoffs are visible without digging through filenames.

  • Conservative filters can overconstrain motion and sacrifice progress when the scene tightens.
  • Less conservative planners can preserve motion but lose safety margin in dense interactions.
  • The proposed CVaR-BF controller is designed to sit between those extremes by preserving feasibility while keeping the probabilistic safety interpretation explicit.

▶ RQ4: Does the safety filter still help when attached to a learned policy on real pedestrian trajectories?

The dataset validation section treats CVaR-BF as a safety layer on top of CrowdNav++, rather than as a replacement for the learned controller.

  • The evaluation intentionally stresses out-of-distribution behavior by increasing the robot radius relative to training.
  • This isolates the question of whether the safety filter can recover safer behavior when the nominal policy alone is no longer reliable.
  • The paired Univ-scenario GIFs let visitors inspect the RL-only rollout and the filtered rollout side by side on the same pedestrian trajectory data.
